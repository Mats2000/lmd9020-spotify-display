# LMD-9020 Spotify Display

An ESP32-DevKitC V4 turns a Sony LMD-9020 into a now-playing screen for
Spotify. It generates colour composite video directly from the ESP32's DAC
and feeds it into the monitor's BNC input.

- **Playing:** the album cover (145×184 pixels) with the song title and
  artist side by side on one line underneath, on a flat background in the
  complementary colour of the cover's dominant hue. With the helper running
  on your Mac, the background becomes a visualizer glowing to the music (see
  [Visualizer](#visualizer)). The next song in the queue has its cover
  downloaded while the current one plays, so it shows the moment the song
  changes; for a song that wasn't next (a skip to elsewhere), its title
  shows on dark while the cover downloads (two or three seconds).
  Only Spotify playing on a computer or a speaker (the Mac, the Echo)
  counts: playing on the phone itself leaves the screensavers up
  (`SPOTIFY_DEVICES` in `include/config.h`; the phone casting to the Echo
  counts as the Echo).
- **Not playing:** the clock and date over a rotating set of screensavers,
  fading to the next every 20 minutes. Particle scenes, tinted from the
  last cover you played:
  - **Waves:** a field of dots rolling like a sea.
  - **Sphere:** a globe of dots, turning and breathing out of round.
  - **Vinyl:** a record of dots seen at an angle, ripples running along the
    grooves and a highlight sweeping across them.
  - **Ridges:** dotted ridge lines rolling toward you, after the *Unknown
    Pleasures* cover.

  Quieter line-art scenes:
  - **Rain:** a bus window at night: out-of-focus street lights sliding
    past, beads of water, drops running down the glass.
  - **Contours:** a survey map of a landscape that slowly reshapes itself,
    with spot heights and fine print.
  - **Currents:** a wind map: fine streaks riding a slowly turning flow.

  And cyber ones, after *Serial Experiments Lain*:
  - **Wired:** utility poles and wires against a dithered dusk, signals
    running along the wires, static, red-dotted shadows, the odd tear.
  - **Network:** the Wired as a living graph: drifting nodes link up,
    packets run along the links.
  - **Tunnel:** a tube of dot rings twisting into cyberspace, data pulses
    running down it.
  - **Scope:** an oscilloscope figure in green phosphor with a beam trail.
  - **Terminal:** hex dumps scrolling past, a blinking prompt.
  - **Static:** TV snow with a rolling band; now and then the signal drops
    out and "present day, present time" flickers in red.
  - **Navi:** a made-up 90s OS desktop, windows opening and closing around
    the clock.
  - **Red Sky:** rooftops, antennas and crows on the wires against a
    blood-red dusk, shadows dotted red.
  - **Psyche:** the clock as a chip's label, circuit traces pulsing outward.

  Built in but switched off in `include/config.h`: VFD (an 80s head unit's
  display), Haze (Gen X soft club) and Crossing (a night street crossing).
- All text is set in **Sora**.
- Everything drifts slowly, and TV snow runs between music and clock, to
  protect the LCD from ghosting (see [Burn-in](#burn-in)).
- **Sleep:** after 20 minutes with nothing playing, the picture fades out and
  the video signal stops completely, so the monitor sees no input. It comes
  back on its own when you play something.

Long titles hold for a moment, then scroll. Pauses shorter than 8 seconds
(and skipping tracks) keep the cover up instead of flashing the clock. The
VFD's spectrum dances to an imaginary song; the ESP32 can't hear what
Spotify plays.

Everything fades: a quick dip to black between tracks, a slower one
between the music and the clock, and a long one between screensavers.

The picture is 256×240 pixels, one byte each: that's the limit of colour
composite generated by an ESP32, with its two framebuffers taking most of
the chip's RAM. Everything but the cover uses a fixed 256-colour palette;
the cover gets 256 colours of its own, picked from that cover, so it needs
only a light dither and comes out smooth rather than grainy.

## Wiring

| ESP32-DevKitC V4 | BNC |
|---|---|
| pin **26** (GPIO26, a DAC) | centre pin |
| **GND** | shell |

With the ESP32 module and antenna at the top and USB at the bottom, both are
on the left-hand header. **26** is the 10th pin down
(`3V3 EN VP VN 34 35 32 33 25 26`), and the nearest **GND** is 4 pins below
it, between 12 and 13. Pin 25 (just above) is the ESP32's other DAC pin and
works too, via `VIDEO_GPIO` in `include/config.h`; this unit's pin 25 was
damaged by a short, so it's set to 26.

That's the whole circuit: no resistors or transistors. A BNC pigtail with
two jumper leads works, or an RCA lead into an RCA-to-BNC adapter. Power the
ESP32 from any USB 5 V supply.

On the monitor:

- Plug into the **composite video input** and select it with the input
  buttons.
- If that input has a 75 Ω / Hi-Z termination switch, set it to **75 Ω**.
  If it has a loop-through output, leave it unconnected.
- Use the **4:3** aspect setting. If you'd rather run it stretched to 16:9,
  see `DISPLAY_PIXEL_ASPECT` in `include/config.h`.
- Edges cut off? Turn on **underscan** if your monitor has it. The layout
  already keeps about 6% clear on every side.

## Setup

You need [PlatformIO](https://platformio.org/install), either the VS Code
extension or the command-line tool (`brew install platformio`).

1. **Wi-Fi.** Copy `include/secrets.example.h` to `include/secrets.h`
   (git-ignored) and fill in your network name and password. The ESP32 only
   does 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, so the network has to broadcast on 2.4 GHz.

2. **Create a Spotify app.** Go to
   [developer.spotify.com/dashboard](https://developer.spotify.com/dashboard),
   log in with your (Premium) Spotify account (the first time, it asks you
   to accept the developer terms) and click **Create app**:
   - App name and description: anything, e.g. "LMD-9020 Display"
   - Redirect URI: `http://127.0.0.1:8888/callback` (exactly this, then
     click **Add**)
   - API: tick **Web API**
   - Agree to the terms and **Save**

   Then open the app's **Settings** and copy the **Client ID**. Click **View
   client secret** and copy that too.

   Since February 2026, Development Mode apps only work if the account that
   owns them has Premium.

3. **Log in once.**

   ```bash
   python3 tools/spotify_auth.py
   ```

   Paste the app's Client ID and Client Secret when asked, approve in the
   browser, and the script writes everything into `include/secrets.h`.

4. **Flash.** Plug in the ESP32 over USB:

   ```bash
   pio run -t upload
   ```

   To watch the log:

   ```bash
   pio device monitor
   ```

   After that, updates can go over Wi-Fi: put any password in `OTA_PASSWORD` in
   `include/secrets.h` (before that first USB flash), then

   ```bash
   tools/ota.sh
   ```

   builds the firmware, finds the display by the broadcast it makes every 2 seconds and
   asks it to update, signed with the password (sent as a hash, never itself). The display
   fetches the firmware from the script, checks it and restarts into it (or name it:
   `tools/ota.sh 192.168.1.82`). It needs the visualizer on (`VISUALIZER 1`, the default).
   macOS may ask once to let Python accept the connection from the board.

On boot you'll see the clock with status messages underneath ("Connecting
to Wi-Fi…", "Setting the clock…") until everything is up. Then start
something in Spotify on any device.

## Customising

- **`include/config.h`:** NTSC/PAL, pixel aspect, timezone (set to New York,
  from this Mac), 12/24-hour clock, blinking colon, date order, poll rates,
  pause grace, which screensavers run and for how long, sleep timeout,
  burn-in drift, cover dithering.
- **Layout, fade speeds, drift amounts:** the constants at the top of
  `src/render/scene.cpp`.
- **The screensavers themselves:** `src/render/savers.cpp`, one function
  each. The imaginary song the VFD moves to is in `src/render/fakeaudio.cpp`.
- **Colours:** `pickPalette()` in `src/render/color.cpp`.
- **Font sizes and weights:** edit `FONTS` in `tools/make_fonts.py`, then
  rerun it with `.venv/bin/python tools/make_fonts.py`. It reads Sora from
  `~/Library/Fonts`.

### Preview without the monitor

The scene code is plain C++, so it also runs on your Mac. The Python tools
need Pillow, so give the project its own environment once, from this
folder:

```bash
python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install pillow fonttools
```

Then:

```bash
.venv/bin/python tools/preview/preview.py
```

That writes PNGs (and with `--gif`, animations) of the now-playing card and
every screensaver to `tools/preview/out/`, stretched to the monitor's pixel
aspect. Use `--cover some.jpg --title "…"
--artist "…"` to try a real cover. It also needs Xcode's command line tools
(for the C++ compiler).

## Visualizer

When Spotify plays on your Mac, **Show Control by Sentia** (the home's lighting desk) listens to it and sends the
music's frequency bands to the display, which then animates the background behind the cover
to the music, through a palette of its own in the cover's colours so it stays smooth on
composite. When the app isn't running, or Spotify plays somewhere else, the card blends back
to its solid background. The app's window shows a live preview of the display, drawn by the
firmware's own scene code, and picks the style:

- **Classic Glow:** after the classic iTunes visualizer (G-Force): the music's own waveform
  (the app sends 64 samples of it with every update) drawn into a picture that flows outward and
  turns, so each frame's lines rush out like a tunnel toward you. The wave shape changes every
  15 seconds or on a big transient: a ring round the cover, spinning spiral arms, oscilloscope
  traces, or lobed loops. Louder music flows faster, turns more and draws bigger.
- **Spectrum:** a 2000s receiver's fluorescent analyzer, split by the cover (lows at the left,
  highs at the right): soft pills of ice-white phosphor behind dark glass, bars that move like
  a meter's needle, peaks that hold then fall away.
- **Pulse Halo:** a glow breathing around the cover, rippling outward on each beat.
- **Lava:** slow, smooth colour waves, quicker and brighter with the music.
- **Rave:** a techno rig locked to the kick, cutting every bar to the next look: a square
  tunnel (frames the cover's shape leaving it on the eighths), laser sheets scanning on the beat,
  an LED wall chasing in sixteenths, thick pillars slamming on with the kick, blinds stepping
  down; a strobe and a ring of light on every kick. Between kicks it keeps counting at the last tempo.
- **Fractal:** after Aleph: a Julia set mirrored twelvefold round the cover, its shape wandering
  with the music, the bass pulsing its zoom, contour lines flowing through it and a ring of light
  on each beat.

**Shuffle** in the style menu picks a new style with every song and every minute.

Next to the style, the app picks the colours, for every style: **Cover Colours** (up to three
of the cover's own strongest hues, dim to bright) or a fixed scheme: **Sentia** (deep purple,
Sentia pink, light blue), **Ice**, **Amber** or **Soft Club**.
- **Off:** the card's normal solid background, even with the app running.

### The Live page

The app opens on **Live**, laid out like a lighting desk, the whole show on one page: what's
connected (display, each light, TILES), the music (tempo, the moment the autopilot reads, level,
a lamp on each kick), the desk, the autopilot and the scenes. The other pages (Lights, Display,
TILES) are for setting things up; **Settings** holds the phone remote, the timing trim and
"Open at login".

- **Master** scales every light's brightness and TILES'.
- **Blackout** puts every light and TILES out until pressed again; the show keeps running
  underneath.
- **Flash** is held: every light, the display's whole screen and TILES (pads and underglow) white
  at full while the button is down. The display and TILES are told again every 50 ms and let go
  by themselves soon after the last, and from a phone it all lets go after 5 s, in case the
  release is lost.
- **Freeze** holds the autopilot on its current look (only with the autopilot on).
- **Scenes** are pads: click one to recall the room, **+** saves the room as it is now (every
  light's look and settings, the display's style) under a name, right-click deletes.

### Lights and Scenes

Lights come along from the same analysis as the display, not a microphone. The **Lights** page
shows every light found; pick one and set it on its own: on or off, brightness, **kick** (how much
each kick flashes it, 0-150%) and its own **look**: a movement (Flow, Twinkle, Flexing, Stacking,
Strike, Gradient, Chase, Pulse), colours (its own, chosen and removed one by one, the cover's or
the display's scheme, in order or at random, changing with the beat or **with the song**: one
colour at a time, the next when the song's character changes, as its energy across the bands and
its loudness over ~1.5 s part from the last ~10 s much more than they usually do in that song),
spots and size (ranges, wider with louder music),
speed, rhythm (brightness from quiet to the beat) and direction. A bulb has no segments, so it
keeps colours, how often they change, and rhythm. **Presets** are looks saved to reuse: one click
copies one into the open light, "Save as a preset" keeps the light's look, "Same on every light"
copies it to the rest.

The **Autopilot** (on the Live page) hands the room to a show director that reads the music once a second (its
loudness against the song's own quiet and loud parts, its character changes, the beat) and picks
a whole-rig look from its style's pool: the lamps' movement and colours, the bulb's style, the
display's visualizer and scheme (and TILES' animation when taken over). **Club** follows the song's
arc (washes in breakdowns, grooves in the body, strobes at the peak, a switch on each drop, a new
look about every 32 bars or at a new section), **Lounge** stays slow and gentle (a change every
minute or two, never a strobe; the bulb drifts slowly through warm hues, low, unless a palette
other than Cover is picked), **Rave** goes maximal (strikes and strobes, a new look every
8 bars). Each style has four macros (Club: energy, pace, colour, drop impact; Lounge: warmth, motion,
depth, drift; Rave: intensity, pace, chaos, strobe), 50 leaving its looks as they are, and a
palette can be picked for the whole rig: Cover, Sentia, Ice, Sunset or One colour, from the picker
beside them (none: each look its own; the display, which only takes its schemes, gets the one
nearest in hue).
It lies over the settings and saves nothing; off, everything is as it was.

Scenes, presets and settings live in `~/Library/Application Support/Show Control`.

- **Govee** lamps are streamed a colour per segment (all 14 on the H6076). Turn on **LAN Control**
  for the lamp in the Govee Home app; the app finds it by itself.
- **Matter** lights (bulbs already in Alexa or Apple Home) play in one of four styles, since a
  bulb only fades slowly: **Strobe** (a hard flash on each kick, straight off between them),
  **Pulse** (up on each kick, fading back down through the beat) or **Wash** (no kicks: brightness
  with the music's energy, colour along the look's colours with how bright or bassy it sounds, in
  long fades) or **Drift** (no kicks: one colour sliding into the next, a minute each at the
  slowest, warm hues unless the look has its own, kept low), through `tools/showcontrol/matter` (needs Node): `npm install` there once, then
  pair each light with the code from its home app's "share" or "add another controller":
  `node lights.js pair 01234567890 bulb`. The helper sends a light one command at a time (a kick
  first), so they can't pile up in the bulb, and reports each light's round trip. build.sh copies
  it to Application Support (macOS keeps an app's helpers out of the Documents folder) and the app
  runs it from there; its output is in `~/Library/Logs/Show Control/matter.log`.

**Alexa keeps the last word.** When a light is turned off from outside the show (Alexa, a routine,
its own app), the show lets go of it and leaves it off, also after a restart, until **Take back**.
**Hand to Alexa** lets go by hand. For a Matter light every "it went off" report is matched to the
show's own offs (the strobe turns it off between kicks): one with no off from the show in the last
1.5 s is someone else's. Colour reports aren't used: the bulb lights pale colours with its white
LEDs and reports "white", and the hue it reports drifts. Ten
seconds after the music stops, or when a light or all lights are switched off or the app quits,
every light the show still has goes back to its own colour and brightness.

**The display's log** comes over Wi-Fi to the Mac running the visualizer (UDP, port 4212) and is
kept in `~/Library/Logs/Show Control/display.log`: each song, each cover (downloaded or from the
prefetch, how long it took, the memory and stack left), every 30 s the render timing.

**On a phone:** the app serves a remote on port 8770. Its **Live** tab is the on-the-go desk: tempo
and moment, the level, Master, Blackout, Flash and Freeze, then the autopilot (its style, what it's
running, Next look, the palette and macros), the scene pads and lights and TILES on or off; then
Lights, Scenes and Display. Set a
password on the Settings page (10 characters or more); a phone signs in once and keeps a long random
key (the Mac keeps only its hash). Five wrong tries lock sign-in for 30 s, doubling each time;
"Sign out every phone" or a new password drops every key. In Safari, Share > Add to Home Screen
makes it an app.

**On the web** (`showcontrol.matiascevallos.com`, from `tools/showcontrol/site`): a Vercel project
serves the same page with a relay behind it, and Show Control reaches out to it, so nothing on the
home network is opened. The Mac keeps one request open to the relay (`api/mac`), which hands over a
tap the moment one is queued (a blocking pop on Upstash Redis, free plan); while a phone is
looking, the Mac posts its state a few times a second. The Mac sends the relay the password's
salt and hash and a key generation, so the relay checks sign-ins itself and gives phones signed
keys; "Sign out every phone" or a new password moves the generation on, and removing the password
removes it from the relay too. The Mac proves itself with a secret stored as `MAC_SECRET` on Vercel
and as `relaySecret` in the app's defaults, beside `relayURL`. `site/deploy.sh` copies the page,
fonts and icon in and deploys. The page only works while the Mac is awake with Show Control
running. A tap takes about 0.4 s there and back.

**Kicks drive everything:** they're found by a small model learned from real songs
(`tools/showcontrol/Drums.swift` and `DrumModel.swift`; how it's made is in
`tools/showcontrol/drums/README.md`). Every 2.7 ms the newest sound is measured in nine bands
(energy, rises over 11 and 32 ms, level against the band's recent peak and the track's loudness),
and the last six steps' measurements give the chance a kick, snare or hat just started. It was
trained against answer keys made offline from the drums Demucs separates out of recordings of
songs played here. On four minutes of songs it hadn't seen, it found 81% of the kicks with 87% of
its finds right, about 2 ms after each started; the earlier hand-made detector found 30%, 36% right,
17 ms after. Snares and hats come through as accents (extra sparkle, never the pulse). The earlier
detector still runs for the tempo the bulb's guesses use. Every sound is timed by when Spotify played it (the
capture's own timestamps), so a light flashes when the kick is *heard*: after the speakers' delay
(Core Audio's figure plus "In time with the speakers"), less the light's own delay (30 ms for a
Govee lamp, half the measured round trip for a Matter light). A light too slow for that (a Matter
bulb takes 100-160 ms a command) gets kicks ahead: a beat grid is fitted through the last 8 s of
kicks, each sixteenth that had a kick in both of the last two bars is guessed to have one again,
and the guesses are sent its own delay early (and 10 ms more) once 70% of them come true; every
light gets them, since the lamp's Wi-Fi is often late too (it dozes between messages, 4-190 ms). A bulb kick goes straight
up and falls back as soon as the bulb has it (with the kick over 100%, straight off with no fade,
"off with effect": the Amazon bulb has no setting for its own slow dimming), and between kicks the bulb sits under half way up
its rhythm (darker still with the kick over 100%), so the flash shows. The Lights page shows the
measured delays. `defaults write com.sentiainstruments.lmd-visualizer logKicks -bool true` logs
every kick to `~/Library/Logs/Show Control/kicks.log` for study.

```sh
.venv/bin/python tools/showcontrol/make_icon.py   # only after changing the icon
tools/showcontrol/build.sh                        # builds tools/showcontrol/build/Show Control.app
cp -R "tools/showcontrol/build/Show Control.app" /Applications/
```

The first time, macOS asks to allow screen and system audio recording (that's how one app's
sound is captured; nothing of the screen is used), control of Spotify (to see whether it's
playing, and for the song and cover in the preview) and access to devices on the local network.
It only records while Spotify plays, so the menu bar's recording indicator goes away about ten
seconds after the music stops (and never shows with the style set to Off). After allowing recording in System
Settings > Privacy & Security, quit and reopen the app. Closing the window keeps it streaming
(click its Dock icon to bring it back; Quit stops it and puts the lights back), and "Open at login" starts it with the
Mac. It finds the display by itself; if your network blocks broadcasts, give it the address:
`defaults write com.sentiainstruments.lmd-visualizer host 192.168.1.82`.

It sends 25 bytes 60 times a second over UDP (port 4210; the display announces itself on
4211). `VISUALIZER 0` in `include/config.h` turns it off on the display.

## Burn-in

LCDs can hold a faint ghost of anything left static and high-contrast, and
the LMD-9020's IPS panel does so readily (the ghost stays even with the cable
unplugged). A clock and a cover are exactly that kind of picture. Three
things fight it:

- **Pixel shift.** The cover card and the screensavers' fixed parts (the
  display frame, the poles, the fine print) drift up to ±5 px sideways and
  ±2 px vertically on a slow Lissajous path, at most a pixel every half
  minute. The clock roams ±5 × ±3 px. The periods share no factors, so the
  path wanders over the whole area instead of retracing.
- **TV snow.** Full-screen snow swings every pixel between black and white,
  which clears a held image: for 5 seconds when the music stops, for the
  last 30 seconds before sleep, and for 15 minutes when you press the board's
  **BOOT** button (press again to stop; it also wakes the display). Times are
  `REFRESH_*` in `include/config.h`.
- **Rotation.** The screensaver changes every `SAVER_MINUTES` (20), so the
  clock moves to a different height and colour and the backgrounds never
  stay the same. Each idle spell carries on with the next screensaver.

`BURNIN_SHIFT 0` in `include/config.h` turns the drift off.

## Sleep

After `SLEEP_AFTER_MINUTES` (20) with nothing playing on Spotify, the
picture fades out over 3 seconds and the ESP32 powers down its DAC, so the
BNC line sits at 0 V with no sync. The monitor treats that as no input;
whether it then shows "no signal" or goes into standby is up to its own
settings. While asleep it also stops making video (Wi-Fi stays awake, so it
keeps hearing from Spotify; if its loop or Spotify go quiet for too long it
restarts itself, staying dark). It keeps polling Spotify, and when
playback starts it powers the signal back up and fades in, usually within
5 to 7 seconds. Set
`SLEEP_AFTER_MINUTES 0` to never sleep.

## How it works

- **Video:** [ESP_8_BIT_composite](https://github.com/Roger-random/ESP_8_BIT_composite)
  (Roger Cheng, built on Peter Barrett's ESP_8_BIT) streams a 256×240 RGB332
  framebuffer through I2S into the DAC (GPIO26 here) as NTSC or PAL colour. It's
  vendored in `lib/ESP_8_BIT_composite` with these changes: the NTSC
  palette is computed at start-up at standard colour strength and hue (the
  original runs at about 43% strength, rotated 27°); one rectangle (the
  cover) can use a palette of its own, switched with the buffers; the PAL
  table lives in flash unless PAL is used, saving 2 KB of RAM; and the
  output is standard 480i NTSC (525 lines interlaced, 227.5 colour cycles a
  line, SMPTE 170M sync, burst and equalizing pulses), the format the
  LMD-9020's composite input is specified for, rather than the original
  240p. The second field sits half a line lower, so it shows each line
  blended with the next; otherwise the monitor's deinterlacer makes every
  horizontal edge flicker (`NTSC_*` in `config.h`). It's
  double-buffered, and each frame is redrawn from scratch at 30 fps (every
  picture held for two fields). Motion follows the clock rather than the
  frame count. Every screensaver draws its frame in 3 to 15 ms of the 33 ms
  budget (measured on the ESP32, with the project built at `-O2`), so frames
  never drop.
- **Two cores:** core 1 only renders (`src/main.cpp`, `src/render/`). Core 0
  handles Wi-Fi, NTP and Spotify (`src/net.cpp`), so a slow HTTPS request
  never stalls the picture.
- **HTTPS:** BearSSL (bundled in the SSLClient library) over a plain
  `WiFiClient`, in `src/https.cpp`, rather than the core's mbedTLS-based
  `WiFiClientSecure`. The framebuffers take ~123 KB of the ESP32's RAM, and
  mbedTLS as built into the Arduino core needs ~55 KB at the peak of a
  handshake; BearSSL needs one 16 KB buffer plus 7 KB of state, allocated
  once. The connection to Spotify is kept alive between polls, and resumed
  sessions skip the expensive part of reconnecting. The trusted roots are
  compiled into flash (`src/trust_anchors.c`).
- **Spotify:** polls `GET /me/player` (what's playing and on which device) every 3 s while
  playing (and again just as the song should end) and every 5 s otherwise. The refresh token lives in flash, and
  Spotify-issued replacements are saved automatically.
- **Covers:** first the 64 px thumbnail is downloaded and split into up to
  256 colours (a variance cut, in `buildArtPalette`). Then the 640 px JPEG
  is decoded straight off the HTTPS stream at half size (TJpgDec, bundled
  with the Arduino core), averaged down to 145×184, mapped to those colours
  with a gentle Floyd–Steinberg dither and analysed for colour in one pass,
  a band of blocks at a time. The compressed file never has to fit in
  memory. The pixels and the palette live in a buffer allocated for
  instruction memory, packed four pixels to a 32-bit word. The cover fades
  by dimming its palette rather than by dithering.
- **The next cover:** when a song starts, and again 40 s before it ends,
  the first song in `GET /me/player/queue` is read (only that one: the rest
  of the reply is never read) and its thumbnail and cover JPEGs are saved to
  the spare 128 KB flash partition (`spiffs` in `min_spiffs.csv`, used raw;
  the 300 px cover when the 640 px one won't fit). When that song comes, the
  cover is decoded from flash, with no download, read straight from the chip a piece at a time
  (through the cache it could come back as the previous cover). Erasing flash pauses the
  render core, so the slot is erased one 4 KB sector at a time between polls
  (`src/prefetch.cpp`).
- **Pixel aspect:** each of the 256 pixels per line is ~1.27× wider than
  tall on a 4:3 picture. The cover is drawn 145×184 so it comes out square,
  and the fonts are pre-squeezed to match.

## Troubleshooting

- **Black and white, or rolling picture:** make sure the monitor is set to
  auto or NTSC, or set `VIDEO_NTSC 0` for PAL.
- **"Spotify login rejected":** rerun `tools/spotify_auth.py` and reflash.
- **"Can't reach Spotify…" with Wi-Fi up:** if the serial log shows TLS
  error 62 or similar on one host, it may have changed certificate
  authority. Check its chain with
  `openssl s_client -connect i.scdn.co:443 -showcerts`, put the root's PEM in
