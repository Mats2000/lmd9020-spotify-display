# LMD-9020 Spotify Display

An ESP32-DevKitC V4 turns a Sony LMD-9020 into a now-playing screen for
Spotify. It generates colour composite video directly from the ESP32's DAC
and feeds it into the monitor's BNC input.

- **Playing:** the album cover (145×184 pixels) with the song title and
  artist side by side on one line underneath, on a flat background in the
  complementary colour of the cover's dominant hue. With the helper running
  on your Mac, the background becomes a visualizer glowing to the music (see
  [Visualizer](#visualizer)). While the next cover downloads (two or three
  seconds), its title shows on dark in the same place.
- **Not playing:** the clock and date over a rotating set of screensavers,
  fading to the next every 20 minutes. Particle scenes, tinted from the
  last cover you played:
  - **Waves:** a field of dots rolling like a sea.
  - **Sphere:** a globe of dots, turning and breathing out of round.
  - **Vinyl:** a record of dots seen at an angle, ripples running along the
    grooves and a highlight sweeping across them.
  - **Ridges:** dotted ridge lines rolling toward you, after the *Unknown
    Pleasures* cover.

  Quieter line-art scenes:
  - **Rain:** a bus window at night: out-of-focus street lights sliding
    past, beads of water, drops running down the glass.
  - **Contours:** a survey map of a landscape that slowly reshapes itself,
    with spot heights and fine print.
  - **Currents:** a wind map: fine streaks riding a slowly turning flow.

  And cyber ones, after *Serial Experiments Lain*:
  - **Wired:** utility poles and wires against a dithered dusk, signals
    running along the wires, static, red-dotted shadows, the odd tear.
  - **Network:** the Wired as a living graph: drifting nodes link up,
    packets run along the links.
  - **Tunnel:** a tube of dot rings twisting into cyberspace, data pulses
    running down it.
  - **Scope:** an oscilloscope figure in green phosphor with a beam trail.
  - **Terminal:** hex dumps scrolling past, a blinking prompt.
  - **Static:** TV snow with a rolling band; now and then the signal drops
    out and "present day, present time" flickers in red.
  - **Navi:** a made-up 90s OS desktop, windows opening and closing around
    the clock.
  - **Red Sky:** rooftops, antennas and crows on the wires against a
    blood-red dusk, shadows dotted red.
  - **Psyche:** the clock as a chip's label, circuit traces pulsing outward.

  Built in but switched off in `include/config.h`: VFD (an 80s head unit's
  display), Haze (Gen X soft club) and Crossing (a night street crossing).
- All text is set in **Sora**.
- Everything drifts slowly, and TV snow runs between music and clock, to
  protect the LCD from ghosting (see [Burn-in](#burn-in)).
- **Sleep:** after 20 minutes with nothing playing, the picture fades out and
  the video signal stops completely, so the monitor sees no input. It comes
  back on its own when you play something.

Long titles hold for a moment, then scroll. Pauses shorter than 8 seconds
(and skipping tracks) keep the cover up instead of flashing the clock. The
VFD's spectrum dances to an imaginary song; the ESP32 can't hear what
Spotify plays.

Everything fades: a quick dip to black between tracks, a slower one
between the music and the clock, and a long one between screensavers.

The picture is 256×240 pixels, one byte each: that's the limit of colour
composite generated by an ESP32, with its two framebuffers taking most of
the chip's RAM. Everything but the cover uses a fixed 256-colour palette;
the cover gets 256 colours of its own, picked from that cover, so it needs
only a light dither and comes out smooth rather than grainy.

## Wiring

| ESP32-DevKitC V4 | BNC |
|---|---|
| pin **26** (GPIO26, a DAC) | centre pin |
| **GND** | shell |

With the ESP32 module and antenna at the top and USB at the bottom, both are
on the left-hand header. **26** is the 10th pin down
(`3V3 EN VP VN 34 35 32 33 25 26`), and the nearest **GND** is 4 pins below
it, between 12 and 13. Pin 25 (just above) is the ESP32's other DAC pin and
works too, via `VIDEO_GPIO` in `include/config.h`; this unit's pin 25 was
damaged by a short, so it's set to 26.

That's the whole circuit: no resistors or transistors. A BNC pigtail with
two jumper leads works, or an RCA lead into an RCA-to-BNC adapter. Power the
ESP32 from any USB 5 V supply.

On the monitor:

- Plug into the **composite video input** and select it with the input
  buttons.
- If that input has a 75 Ω / Hi-Z termination switch, set it to **75 Ω**.
  If it has a loop-through output, leave it unconnected.
- Use the **4:3** aspect setting. If you'd rather run it stretched to 16:9,
  see `DISPLAY_PIXEL_ASPECT` in `include/config.h`.
- Edges cut off? Turn on **underscan** if your monitor has it. The layout
  already keeps about 6% clear on every side.

## Setup

You need [PlatformIO](https://platformio.org/install), either the VS Code
extension or the command-line tool (`brew install platformio`).

1. **Wi-Fi.** Copy `include/secrets.example.h` to `include/secrets.h`
   (git-ignored) and fill in your network name and password. The ESP32 only
   does 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, so the network has to broadcast on 2.4 GHz.

2. **Create a Spotify app.** Go to
   [developer.spotify.com/dashboard](https://developer.spotify.com/dashboard),
   log in with your (Premium) Spotify account (the first time, it asks you
   to accept the developer terms) and click **Create app**:
   - App name and description: anything, e.g. "LMD-9020 Display"
   - Redirect URI: `http://127.0.0.1:8888/callback` (exactly this, then
     click **Add**)
   - API: tick **Web API**
   - Agree to the terms and **Save**

   Then open the app's **Settings** and copy the **Client ID**. Click **View
   client secret** and copy that too.

   Since February 2026, Development Mode apps only work if the account that
   owns them has Premium.

3. **Log in once.**

   ```bash
   python3 tools/spotify_auth.py
   ```

   Paste the app's Client ID and Client Secret when asked, approve in the
   browser, and the script writes everything into `include/secrets.h`.

4. **Flash.** Plug in the ESP32 over USB:

   ```bash
   pio run -t upload
   ```

   To watch the log:

   ```bash
   pio device monitor
   ```

On boot you'll see the clock with status messages underneath ("Connecting
to Wi-Fi…", "Setting the clock…") until everything is up. Then start
something in Spotify on any device.

## Customising

- **`include/config.h`:** NTSC/PAL, pixel aspect, timezone (set to New York,
  from this Mac), 12/24-hour clock, blinking colon, date order, poll rates,
  pause grace, which screensavers run and for how long, sleep timeout,
  burn-in drift, cover dithering.
- **Layout, fade speeds, drift amounts:** the constants at the top of
  `src/render/scene.cpp`.
- **The screensavers themselves:** `src/render/savers.cpp`, one function
  each. The imaginary song the VFD moves to is in `src/render/fakeaudio.cpp`.
- **Colours:** `pickPalette()` in `src/render/color.cpp`.
- **Font sizes and weights:** edit `FONTS` in `tools/make_fonts.py`, then
  rerun it with `.venv/bin/python tools/make_fonts.py`. It reads Sora from
  `~/Library/Fonts`.

### Preview without the monitor

The scene code is plain C++, so it also runs on your Mac. The Python tools
need Pillow, so give the project its own environment once, from this
folder:

```bash
python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install pillow fonttools
```

Then:

```bash
.venv/bin/python tools/preview/preview.py
```

That writes PNGs (and with `--gif`, animations) of the now-playing card and
every screensaver to `tools/preview/out/`, stretched to the monitor's pixel
aspect. Use `--cover some.jpg --title "…"
--artist "…"` to try a real cover. It also needs Xcode's command line tools
(for the C++ compiler).

## Visualizer

When Spotify plays on your Mac, the **LMD Visualizer** app there listens to it and sends the
music's frequency bands to the display, which then animates the background behind the cover
to the music, through a palette of its own in the cover's colours so it stays smooth on
composite. When the app isn't running, or Spotify plays somewhere else, the card blends back
to its solid background. The app's window shows a live preview of the display, drawn by the
firmware's own scene code, and picks the style:

- **Classic Glow:** loops swirling out in soft feedback trails, after the classic iTunes look.
- **Spectrum:** a 2000s receiver's fluorescent analyzer, split by the cover (lows at the left,
  highs at the right): soft pills of ice-white phosphor behind dark glass, bars that move like
  a meter's needle, peaks that hold then fall away.
- **Pulse Halo:** a glow breathing around the cover, rippling outward on each beat.
- **Lava:** slow, smooth colour waves, quicker and brighter with the music.
- **Off:** the card's normal solid background, even with the app running.

```sh
.venv/bin/python tools/visualizer/make_icon.py   # only after changing the icon
tools/visualizer/build.sh                        # builds tools/visualizer/build/LMD Visualizer.app
cp -R "tools/visualizer/build/LMD Visualizer.app" /Applications/
```

The first time, macOS asks to allow screen and system audio recording (that's how one app's
sound is captured; nothing of the screen is used), control of Spotify (to see whether it's
playing, and for the song and cover in the preview) and access to devices on the local network.
It only records while Spotify plays, so the menu bar's recording indicator goes away about ten
seconds after the music stops (and never shows with the style set to Off). After allowing recording in System
Settings > Privacy & Security, quit and reopen the app. Closing the window keeps it streaming
(click its Dock icon to bring it back; Quit stops it), and "Open at login" starts it with the
Mac. It finds the display by itself; if your network blocks broadcasts, give it the address:
`defaults write com.sentiainstruments.lmd-visualizer host 192.168.1.82`.

It sends 25 bytes 60 times a second over UDP (port 4210; the display announces itself on
4211). `VISUALIZER 0` in `include/config.h` turns it off on the display.

## Burn-in

LCDs can hold a faint ghost of anything left static and high-contrast, and
the LMD-9020's IPS panel does so readily (the ghost stays even with the cable
unplugged). A clock and a cover are exactly that kind of picture. Three
things fight it:

- **Pixel shift.** The cover card and the screensavers' fixed parts (the
  display frame, the poles, the fine print) drift up to ±10 px sideways and
  ±3 px vertically on a slow Lissajous path, at most a pixel every half
  minute. The clock roams ±10 × ±6 px. The periods share no factors, so the
  path wanders over the whole area instead of retracing.
- **TV snow.** Full-screen snow swings every pixel between black and white,
  which clears a held image: for 15 seconds when the music stops, for the
  last minute before sleep, and for 30 minutes when you press the board's
  **BOOT** button (press again to stop; it also wakes the display). Times are
  `REFRESH_*` in `include/config.h`.
- **Rotation.** The screensaver changes every `SAVER_MINUTES` (20), so the
  clock moves to a different height and colour and the backgrounds never
  stay the same. Each idle spell carries on with the next screensaver.

`BURNIN_SHIFT 0` in `include/config.h` turns the drift off.

## Sleep

After `SLEEP_AFTER_MINUTES` (20) with nothing playing on Spotify, the
picture fades out over 3 seconds and the ESP32 powers down its DAC, so the
BNC line sits at 0 V with no sync. The monitor treats that as no input;
whether it then shows "no signal" or goes into standby is up to its own
settings. While asleep it also stops making video, slows its CPU to 80 MHz
and lets Wi-Fi doze between beacons. It keeps polling Spotify, and when
playback starts it powers the signal back up and fades in, usually within
5 to 7 seconds. Set
`SLEEP_AFTER_MINUTES 0` to never sleep.

## How it works

- **Video:** [ESP_8_BIT_composite](https://github.com/Roger-random/ESP_8_BIT_composite)
  (Roger Cheng, built on Peter Barrett's ESP_8_BIT) streams a 256×240 RGB332
  framebuffer through I2S into the DAC (GPIO26 here) as NTSC or PAL colour. It's
  vendored in `lib/ESP_8_BIT_composite` with these changes: the NTSC
  palette is computed at start-up at standard colour strength and hue (the
  original runs at about 43% strength, rotated 27°); one rectangle (the
  cover) can use a palette of its own, switched with the buffers; the PAL
  table lives in flash unless PAL is used, saving 2 KB of RAM; and the
  output is standard 480i NTSC (525 lines interlaced, 227.5 colour cycles a
  line, SMPTE 170M sync, burst and equalizing pulses), the format the
  LMD-9020's composite input is specified for, rather than the original
  240p. The second field sits half a line lower, so it shows each line
  blended with the next; otherwise the monitor's deinterlacer makes every
  horizontal edge flicker (`NTSC_*` in `config.h`). It's
  double-buffered, and each frame is redrawn from scratch at 30 fps (every
  picture held for two fields). Motion follows the clock rather than the
  frame count. Every screensaver draws its frame in 3 to 15 ms of the 33 ms
  budget (measured on the ESP32, with the project built at `-O2`), so frames
  never drop.
- **Two cores:** core 1 only renders (`src/main.cpp`, `src/render/`). Core 0
  handles Wi-Fi, NTP and Spotify (`src/net.cpp`), so a slow HTTPS request
  never stalls the picture.
- **HTTPS:** BearSSL (bundled in the SSLClient library) over a plain
  `WiFiClient`, in `src/https.cpp`, rather than the core's mbedTLS-based
  `WiFiClientSecure`. The framebuffers take ~123 KB of the ESP32's RAM, and
  mbedTLS as built into the Arduino core needs ~55 KB at the peak of a
  handshake; BearSSL needs one 16 KB buffer plus 7 KB of state, allocated
  once. The connection to Spotify is kept alive between polls, and resumed
  sessions skip the expensive part of reconnecting. The trusted roots are
  compiled into flash (`src/trust_anchors.c`).
- **Spotify:** polls `GET /me/player/currently-playing` every 3 s while
  playing and every 5 s otherwise. The refresh token lives in flash, and
  Spotify-issued replacements are saved automatically.
- **Covers:** first the 64 px thumbnail is downloaded and split into up to
  256 colours (a variance cut, in `buildArtPalette`). Then the 640 px JPEG
  is decoded straight off the HTTPS stream at half size (TJpgDec, bundled
  with the Arduino core), averaged down to 145×184, mapped to those colours
  with a gentle Floyd–Steinberg dither and analysed for colour in one pass,
  a band of blocks at a time. The compressed file never has to fit in
  memory. The pixels and the palette live in a buffer allocated for
  instruction memory, packed four pixels to a 32-bit word. The cover fades
  by dimming its palette rather than by dithering.
- **Pixel aspect:** each of the 256 pixels per line is ~1.27× wider than
  tall on a 4:3 picture. The cover is drawn 145×184 so it comes out square,
  and the fonts are pre-squeezed to match.

## Troubleshooting

- **Black and white, or rolling picture:** make sure the monitor is set to
  auto or NTSC, or set `VIDEO_NTSC 0` for PAL.
- **"Spotify login rejected":** rerun `tools/spotify_auth.py` and reflash.
- **"Can't reach Spotify…" with Wi-Fi up:** if the serial log shows TLS
  error 62 or similar on one host, it may have changed certificate
  authority. Check its chain with
  `openssl s_client -connect i.scdn.co:443 -showcerts`, put the root's PEM in
  `tools/certs/`, and rerun `.venv/bin/python tools/make_trust_anchors.py`
  (needs `.venv/bin/pip install cryptography`).
- **A screensaver looks choppy:** the serial log reports frame times every
  30 s. "Over budget" means a frame took longer than 33 ms.
- **Crashes or "Out of memory":** the serial log prints free heap at each
  track change; it should sit around 25 KB.
